3B · Where CPH & ACD sit vs the comparable set CPH ACD

CPH vs large halls (Woman's Club, City Hall Rotunda, Starlite, Corinthian, Villa Ragusa)

  • Hours: large halls commonly bundle 8 hours; CPH's 6-hour base sits below that norm — a point to weigh when positioning value.
  • Overtime: comparables that publish it charge $300–$500/hr; CPH's 1.5× multiplier is in range once its base rate is filled in.
  • Price band: CPH's $3,845/$4,845 all-in sits between the civic/community halls ($2,000–$5,200) and the catering-driven ballrooms (F&B minimums $5,500–$12,000). Competitive, and more transparent than the F&B-minimum model.
  • Deposit: comparables use a refundable $500–$1,500 deposit; CPH's refundable security deposit matches that practice (its nonrefundable retainer is the part to make defensible).

ACD vs small/studio spaces (Prestige, Fuze, Giggster studio, Santa Teresa patio)

  • Hourly norm: event rooms cluster around $250–$300/hr with a 3–6-hour minimum; ACD's $200/$300-per-hour add-on rates are right in band.
  • BYO bar: ACD's BYO model matches Fuze and the civic halls — but those all gate BYO on a licensed bartender and/or liquor-liability insurance, which ACD should state explicitly.
  • Insurance: ≥$1M renter liability is a common hard requirement (both Giggster listings, the civic halls); ACD's "may require" is softer than the market.
  • Transparency: comparables publish a single clear hourly or flat figure; ACD's advertised-vs-supplied gap is out of step with how these competitors present price.

A shared policy architecture for both venues — same stages, same deposit vocabulary, same mechanics — with rates and operational rules kept venue-specific. Every proposal below is a draft for management review; items touching enforceability also carry a legal-review flag.

8A · Deposit architecture — four clearly separated roles

Reservation payment

Secures the date. May be applied to rent if that is the approved arrangement. One defined role — not also the security deposit.

Rental payment

Pays for the agreed booking. Due on a stated schedule with a clear final-balance deadline.

Refundable security deposit

Secures post-event obligations; returned less documented, itemized deductions within a stated window.

Rescheduling credit

A defined credit under approved conditions and a stated expiry — presented as a credit, not a forfeiture trap.

ACD fix. Today one $1,000 plays all four roles. Either (a) split it into a reservation payment + a separate refundable security deposit, or (b) keep one amount but define a single, counsel-reviewed conversion with clean accounting at each stage and no stacked retention. Recommended: option (a) — easiest to explain, account for, and defend.

8B · Cancellation model — graduated, rebooking-aware

Chosen because it tracks actual loss (the harder a date is to rebook, the more is retained), which is what Civil Code §1671(d) requires of a consumer-contract forfeiture — not because it is common. Exact percentages are a ⚖️ + 🔵 item.

≥ 180 days
Refund all rental payments; retain only the reservation payment (or a stated, reasonable processing amount). Date is highly rebookable.
90–179 days
Retain reservation payment + a defined percentage of rental payments reflecting partial rebooking risk; refund the remainder within 30 days.
30–89 days
Higher retention; offer a rescheduling credit as the preferred alternative to cash forfeiture.
< 30 days
Retain the most — committed costs + reasonable estimate — but still no stacking of a flat amount on top of a full-payment retention (avoids duplicative recovery).
Key legal principle. Retaining money and claiming further damages for the same loss risks duplicative recovery; the forfeiture must be a reasonable endeavor to estimate actual damages or it is void as a penalty. Counsel sets the defensible percentages.
