CRM Lead-Scoring Research & Redesign · October 2026

A Buying-Intent Lead Score for CPH & ACD

Crown Prince Event Hall (CPH) · Alvin Studio & Courtyard (ACD) · event-venue CRM · scoring methodology + UI specification + prototype

A redesign of the lead score so it measures demonstrated buying intent and real progress toward a booking — not form completeness. Written to be handed directly to the CRM developer as scoring rules, backend logic, UI specs and test cases. A clickable prototype accompanies this document.

Read first — scope and honesty of the number. The figure this model produces is a weighted buying-intent priority score out of 100. It is not a statistically validated probability that a lead will book. Those are different things (§4, §5). Every sample score, conversion rate and threshold in this document is illustrative until it is recalibrated against CPH/ACD's own historical bookings (§10). Point values are starting assumptions to validate with data, not industry constants.

1 · Executive summary

The current model scores a lead mostly on how completely its profile is filled in and whether staff performed administrative actions (a phone call, a bar/catering question answered). It barely registers what the customer did. The result is backwards: a stranger who fills in a tidy form outscores a serious buyer who booked a tour the moment they inquired.

Recommended direction — a two-axis, lifecycle-aware score with classification overrides:

  • Intent & behaviour is the dominant axis (55 of 100 points). Points are driven by the furthest booking milestone the customer reaches — responded → tour booked → tour completed → quote → booking-intent — so the score rises as a real buyer progresses and cannot be gamed by filling in fields.
  • Fit / qualification is the secondary axis (35 points) — event type, serviceable date, guest count within capacity, date availability, budget alignment. Unknown fields score 0, never a penalty.
  • Engagement quality (10 points) is the only pool that decays with silence, so an old reply doesn't keep a dead lead warm.
  • Negative signals (no-show, repeated non-response) are capped and mostly reversible; hard disqualifiers move the lead to a Lost lifecycle state rather than just denting the score.
  • Classification overrides guarantee the behaviour the business cares about: a self-booked Calendly tour makes a lead at least Warm even with an empty profile; a deposit discussion makes it Hot.
  • "Not scored yet" is a real state for new/uncontacted leads with too little information — shown with the missing fields and a next action, never a misleading low percentage that reads as "Cold".
  • A booking is a conversion, not a 100-point prospect. Deposit paid / agreement signed moves the lead to a Won lifecycle state and retires it from active prospect scoring.

The UI redesign turns the score into a decision aid: a compact card showing score, classification, tour status, last activity and the single next action; a click-to-open breakdown that separates positive signals, attention items and genuinely unknown fields; a score-vs-event timeline; and a prioritisation dashboard that ranks by urgency, not score alone. Everything is specified to reuse existing CRM lead and event records, compute on the server in California time, and keep weights in configuration so they can be tuned without a rewrite.

2 · Current model audit — keep, change, drop

The current 100-point model and its known problems, assessed factor by factor.

Current factorNowVerdictWhy
Guest count (200+ = full 30)30ChangeOver-weighted, and "bigger is better" is wrong for a venue. A 300-guest wedding is not 5× more valuable than a 120-guest one that fills a weekend. Re-cast as capacity & package fit worth 8, per venue.

Missing entirely — the costly gaps: tour booking (esp. self-booked Calendly), tour attendance / no-show, quotation requests, deposit & agreement discussion, two-way responsiveness, and any lifecycle handling for Won / Lost. These are exactly the behaviours that predict a booking.

The six stated problems are all real and all trace to one root cause: the model scores what the office recorded, not what the customer did. It cannot tell apart a lead who was never contacted, a lead who went quiet, and a lead actively closing — because it never looks at customer behaviour. The redesign fixes the cause, not the weights.

3 · Research & best practice

Five established approaches, with their fit for a small event-venue sales team. Vendor sources below describe methods credibly; the specific point values they quote are examples, not standards — a point made repeatedly in the literature itself.

ApproachWhat it scoresStrengthWeakness / requirement
Explicit / fitStatic attributes: event type, guest count, date, budget, capacity suitabilityAvailable immediately; cheap; qualifies ICPSays nothing about intent. A perfect-fit lead with no engagement is not sales-ready.6
Behavioural / intentTour bookings, quote requests, availability questions, booking actionsThe best near-term predictor of a booking; hard to fakeNeeds reliable event capture in the CRM; nothing to score before first contact.
EngagementReplies, two-way conversations, response speed, repeat contactShows the relationship is live; speed matters hugely7Needs trustworthy timestamps; must decay or stale activity looks hot.4
Lifecycle / milestoneProgress: inquiry → qualified → tour → quote → deposit → bookedNaturally avoids double-counting; weights rise with progress; maps to the sales process8Requires agreed stage definitions shared by the whole team.
Predictive (ML)Historical outcomes → modelled probability of bookingMost accurate once trained; self-calibratingNeeds volume. Salesforce Einstein cites on the order of 1,000 leads and 120 conversions before it is reliable.3 A single venue may take 1–2 years to reach that.

What the evidence says

  • Score fit and engagement on two axes, then read them together. The most durable models keep a fit score and a behaviour/engagement score separate and act on the combination — the classic "A1" grid (fit graded A–D, interest 1–4; A1 = best).5 Neither axis alone is enough.6 Established best practice.
  • Explicit is static, implicit (behaviour) is dynamic; the strongest leads score on both.1 Established best practice.
  • Decay behavioural points so recent activity outweighs old. Decay keeps a score tied to current engagement; without it, stale actions keep a dead lead looking hot.4 Common example: reduce engagement points over ~30 days.1 Established best practice.
  • Negative scoring has a role but needs a floor. Unsubscribes / disinterest should reduce a score; practitioners disagree on whether to allow negatives, so cap the downside and let a returning lead recover quickly.4 Established, with a documented debate.
  • Speed to respond is decisive. HBR's Short Life of Online Sales Leads found firms contacting a lead within an hour were ~7× more likely to have a qualifying conversation; a separate Lead Response Management study reported the odds of qualifying a lead were ~21× higher at 5 minutes than at 30.7 These are relative odds, not conversion rates. Evidence; informs the "respond fast" next-action, not the score directly.
  • Review weights regularly (≈ quarterly) against outcomes.4 Established best practice.

Our recommendation vs. the evidence. The evidence backs a two-axis, decaying, lifecycle-aligned model. Our specific choices — intent weighted at 55/100, the milestone ladder values, a Calendly booking forcing Warm, Hot ≥ 70 — are reasoned starting points tuned to this business, flagged throughout as "validate with data." We do not recommend a predictive ML model yet: the data volume isn't there. §10 defines the path to it.

4 · Recommended scoring architecture

Four candidate architectures were considered against the brief's explicit warning about inflating scores when only one or two factors are known.

OptionBehaviourRisk
Fixed weighted sum / 100Points earned out of a fixed 100; absence = 0New leads always look low → read as "Cold". The current model's core flaw.
Normalised over known factorsScore = earned ÷ availableInflation: one known positive field → 100%. Dangerous with sparse data.
Separate fit + behaviour scoresTwo numbers, read as a gridAccurate but two numbers are harder to show on a dense Kanban card.
Hybrid: one fixed-weight score + overrides + lifecycle states + a separate confidence indicator ✓One 0–100 priority number for the card; classification governed by score and override rules and lifecycle; a distinct data-readiness gauge decides "Not scored yet"Slightly more logic — but it is the only option that shows one clean number without either penalising new leads or inflating thin ones.

Recommended: the hybrid. It resolves the central tension directly:

  • The priority score is a fixed-weight sum (max 100) of positive earned points minus a capped negative pool. Unknown ≠ negative; it is simply 0 earned, so no inflation from normalisation.
  • Not scored yet is decided by a separate data-readiness / confidence gauge (how many key fields are known), not by the low score — so a sparse new lead is "Not scored yet", not "Cold".
  • Classification overrides and lifecycle states catch the cases a raw number gets wrong (Calendly booking → Warm floor; deposit → Hot; disinterest → Lost).

Internally this is still the two-axis model the evidence supports — fit and intent are computed separately (and both are shown in the breakdown and used by the dashboard grid) — but the card surfaces a single governed number.

What the percentage means — stated plainly

The headline number is a buying-intent priority score: "how strongly should a salesperson prioritise this lead right now." It is ordinal guidance, not a probability. A 90 is not "90% likely to book"; it means "well ahead of a 45 in the queue." To turn the score into a calibrated probability of booking requires training on CPH/ACD's own closed outcomes — see the migration path in §10. Until then the UI labels it priority, never probability.

6 · Hot / Warm / Cold classification rules

Thresholds are tuned so the worked examples (§7) land sensibly, then recalibrated against real conversion data (§10). Starting ranges:

ClassScoreMeaningEnters when
●Hot70–100Priority — act todayTour done + quoting/booking-intent, or full fit + active + quote
◐Warm40–69Qualified / promising — keep engagingTour booked, or good fit + some engagement
○Cold0–39Low current priority — re-engage or let restWorked but low intent, or negative lifecycle
◇Not scored—Too little info for a meaningful scoreNew / uncontacted, confidence below gate, no high-intent action yet

Critical rule — Cold is earned, not defaulted

A lead is Cold only if it has been worked (contacted and given a real chance to respond) and still shows low intent, or it hit a negative lifecycle event. A brand-new or never-contacted lead is Not scored yet — shown with its missing fields and a next action, never a red "Cold" that blames the lead for the office not calling.

Classification overrides (applied after the raw score)

  1. Calendly tour booked (not since cancelled/no-showed) → floor = Warm. The brief's headline requirement: an immediate self-booked tour is a strong buying signal regardless of empty qualification fields.
  2. Tour completed → floor = Warm (and typically scores into Hot).
  3. Booking-intent milestone (deposit/agreement discussion) → floor = Hot.
  4. Explicit disinterest / disqualified → ceiling = Cold and move to Lost lifecycle, overriding any high score.
  5. Active, unrecovered no-show on a lead otherwise below Warm → may not display Hot until re-engaged.

Lifecycle states — separate from the score

Lifecycle is an operational status that runs alongside (not instead of) the score. It mirrors the CRM's existing Journey: Lead → Contacted → Tour → Proposal → Deposit → Won / Lost / Disqualified (confirmed in §8). The score layers on top of the Journey the team already clicks through — it does not replace it.

  • Won (Booked): deposit paid or agreement signed → lead is a completed conversion. Score is frozen and the lead leaves active prospect prioritisation, shown with a "Booked" badge. Deposit and signature are the exit, not ordinary scoring factors applied to an already-converted lead.
  • Lost / Disqualified: archived with a reason, classified Cold, excluded from active queues (still available for backtesting and re-engagement campaigns).

7 · Automated calculation & worked examples

The formula

fit        = min(35, Σ fit_points_for_known_fields)      // unknown fields contribute 0
intent     = milestone_base(highest_reached)
             + min(bonus_cap, Σ proactivity_bonuses)     // whole block capped at 55
engagement = decay(active_pts + speed_pts, days_since_last_reply)   // 0..10, never < 0
negatives  = min(25, Σ active_reversible_penalties)      // reversible; excludes never-contacted
raw        = clamp(fit + intent + engagement - negatives, 0, 100)

classification =
  if lifecycle in {Won, Lost, Disqualified}      -> lifecycle badge (not Hot/Warm/Cold queue)
  else if confidence < GATE and no high_intent    -> "Not scored yet"
  else apply_overrides(band(raw))                 // band: 70+/40-69/<40 ; overrides set floors/ceilings
confidence = known_key_fields / key_fields        // event type, date, guests, budget, contact

All of this runs server-side (§8) so desktop and mobile always agree, and all date math uses America/Los_Angeles.

8 · Automation & backend requirements

Compute on the server, on every relevant change

Scoring is a pure server-side function of the lead's current fields + its event log. Recalculate (idempotently) on each trigger:

  • Inquiry created
  • Event details added / changed
  • Tour booked / rescheduled / cancelled / completed / no-show
  • Customer message or reply recorded
  • Quotation requested / created / revised
  • Budget or package updated
  • Booking requirements / deposit discussed
  • Deposit recorded · agreement signed · booking confirmed / cancelled

Plus a nightly job for the only time-based effects — engagement decay and no-show penalty restoration — so a score can change with the passage of time without a user action.

Grounded in the existing CRM. A video-tour transcript of the live CRM (captured 15 Oct 2026; web-crm-video-tour-transcript.md in this repo) confirms every record this engine reads already exists — so the model reuses existing data and invents no new capture:

Journey stages: Lead → Contacted → Tour → Proposal → Deposit → WonIntent milestone ladder (§5B) & lifecycle states (§6) — near 1:1
Communications — Text/Email thread attached to each leadEngagement & responsiveness (§5C); customer-reply = the "Engaged" milestone
Interaction Recap after each call/textTwo-way engagement quality; a logged recap evidences a real exchange
Quotation on venue template, with a 72-hour deposit linkQuotation milestone (+ the date-hold / negotiation proactivity bonus)
Payments — deposit received (e.g. Zelle, bank-checked)Booking-intent → Won conversion exit (§6)

Persistence, audit & configuration

  • On the lead record: score, classification, confidence, fit_sub, intent_sub, engagement_sub, lifecycle_state, top_positive[], top_attention[], unknown_fields[], next_action, scored_at.
  • Append-only score_events log (the audit + timeline source): event_id (idempotency key), lead_id, type, delta, score_before, score_after, reason, source, occurred_at. Dedupe on event_id; collapse duplicate inbound messages in a short window. This is what stops repeated/automated events inflating the score and what powers the trend timeline.
  • Weights & thresholds live in a versioned config table, not in code — so staff/analyst can retune after backtesting without a rewrite, and each score row records which config version produced it.
  • Per-venue parameters (capacity, package budget bands) keyed by venue so CPH and ACD share one engine with different fit rules.
  • Tests: unit tests per factor; the five §7 examples as golden fixtures; idempotency test (same event twice → one delta); decay test (advance the clock); override tests; a backtest harness (§10).

9 · CRM UI recommendations

Principles: score and classification are visually distinct from the operational pipeline stage (a lead can be in "Contacted" yet score Hot); meaning is never carried by colour alone (every state pairs a glyph + label); show the few things that drive a decision, hide the rest behind a click. See §12 for the full visual system; this is the functional contract.

  • Pipeline card surfaces: name + event type · event date & days-out · score (ring) · classification chip · tour status · last meaningful activity · one next action. Nothing else.
  • Click the score → breakdown drawer: current score & class, strongest positives, attention/risks, still-unknown fields (clearly not the same as risks), per-factor contribution, recent score-changing events, recommended next action.
  • Trend timeline ties score changes to the CRM events that caused them.
  • Explainability is mandatory: every score shows its top positive and top negative drivers in plain language, sourced from top_positive[] / top_attention[].
  • All Leads stays readable: show Hot/Warm chips only (Cold and Not-scored unlabelled or muted), exactly as today.

10 · Testing & validation plan

  • Backtest first. Replay historical leads through the engine using only data known at each point in time; compare the score/class the model would have given against who actually booked. Guard against look-ahead leakage (don't let a known booking inflate its own earlier score).
  • Core metrics by class: booking conversion rate for Hot / Warm / Cold (Hot should convert markedly higher or the model is miscalibrated); tour-booking & attendance rate by class; lead→quote and lead→booking rates; time-to-booking.
  • Error analysis: false positives (Hot that never booked) and false negatives (Cold/low that booked — the expensive misses); inspect what the model missed.
  • Trajectory: how scores move across the journey; does a rising score precede bookings.
  • Bias watch: because the score partly reflects staff follow-up, monitor for leads scored low only because no one contacted them, and for differences by venue or staff member. Missing-data patterns must not become a hidden penalty.
  • Cadence: review weights quarterly against accumulated outcomes;4 keep a config-version changelog.

When is it a probability? Only after enough closed outcomes to fit and validate a model and show the predicted probabilities match reality (calibration). As a reference point, Salesforce Einstein cites ~1,000 leads with ~120 conversions before predictive scoring is reliable.3 A two-venue operation will likely need 12–24 months of clean data to approach that. Until then, present and label the number as a priority score — never as a conversion probability.

12.B · Score & colour system

Accessible, and never colour-only — each class has a glyph + label:

  • ●Hot — filled circle · wine · highest priority
  • ◐Warm — half circle · gold · promising
  • ○Cold — open circle · slate · low priority
  • ◇Not scored — dashed · neutral grey · needs info

Behaviour on change (restrained — no flashing, no dramatic motion, honouring prefers-reduced-motion):

  • Score up: ring fills with a brief ≤300ms ease; a small green "▲ +N" appears for the session.
  • Score down: ring eases down; muted wine "▼ −N"; no alarm.
  • Class change: chip cross-fades; the change is written to the timeline.
  • High-intent action (Calendly booking): tour chip appears + a one-line "Buying signal: tour booked" note; card may sort up.
  • Negative (no-show): amber attention chip; score dips but penalty is marked temporary in the breakdown.
  • Insufficient info: no number — the "Not scored" chip + a data-readiness meter.

12.H · Mobile

Mobile-first, for staff in the field: a compact card (ring + class + date + tour + one next action + quick call/text/email buttons), a bottom-sheet breakdown (the §12.C zones, stacked, charts simplified to bars), a condensed timeline (event list, line chart hidden), and follow-up / upcoming-tour reminders up top. Keep: score, class, next action, breakdown list, activity list. Simplify/hide: multi-series charts and the dashboard's wide conversion visuals. Because scoring is server-side, the number and class are identical to desktop.

12.I · Visual direction

Modern, professional, minimal — the editorial system of this document (Bodoni Moda headings, Source Sans body, IBM Plex Mono for data), restrained wine/gold/slate accents, subtle borders, accessible contrast, reusable components, responsive. No redundant KPI tiles, no gradients/heavy shadows, no oversized charts, and nothing that duplicates information already in the CRM. The redesign improves the existing pipeline and All-Leads views; it does not touch unrelated modules or the lifecycle stages.

Final recommendation

Replace the completeness-weighted score with a two-axis, lifecycle-aware buying-intent priority score: intent/behaviour dominant (55), fit secondary (35), decaying engagement (10), capped reversible negatives, classification overrides (Calendly → Warm, deposit → Hot), a genuine "Not scored yet" state for thin new leads, and Won/Lost lifecycle handling so a booking is treated as a conversion, not a prospect. Compute it server-side on event triggers in California time, log every change idempotently for audit and the trend timeline, and keep weights in config for quarterly retuning. Surface it through the redesigned card, breakdown drawer, timeline and prioritisation dashboard specified in §12 and shown in the prototype. Call the number a priority score, not a probability, until it has been backtested and calibrated against CPH/ACD's own bookings — the point at which, with enough data, a predictive model can run alongside it. Build in the five phases of §11; Phase 1 alone fixes the brief's headline failures within the existing CRM.

Lead-scoring methods are well documented; the specific point values and thresholds in these sources are examples, not standards — as several of the sources themselves note. Primary studies are distinguished from vendor guidance.

  2. Control Lead Score Durations with Decay & Timeframe Settings — decay vs. timeframe (vendor guide).
  3. Salesforce Einstein AI for Sales: Lead Scoring Guide — predictive data thresholds (~1,000 leads / 120 conversions); verify against current Salesforce docs (secondary).
  4. Lead scoring best practices with Mautic — decay for inactivity, negative scoring, score floors (vendor docs); corroborated by the Adobe Marketo community thread on flooring/resetting scores.
  5. Lead scoring and grading (A1 grid) — fit (A–D) × interest (1–4) matrix (industry reference).
  6. Marketing Qualified Lead Criteria: Scoring & SLA — fit and engagement both required; composite thresholds (vendor guide).

Separate Lead Response Management / InsideSales (2007) study: ~21× qualify odds at 5 vs 30 min (relative odds, not conversion rates) — see speed-to-lead summary.
  8. Why Align Lead Scoring with Lifecycle Stages — same behaviour means different things at different stages (vendor guide).
