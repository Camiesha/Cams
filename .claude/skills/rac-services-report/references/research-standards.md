## Finding the field — how candidates get chosen

The verdict can only be as good as the field it beat. "Find 5–7 yourself" is never permission to take the first page of Google.

**Decompose the job first.** Break the hire into the attributes that define what "comparable" means: trade or profession, service area, job scale, price band, and the engagement model (one-off job, ongoing service, subscription). A candidate outside them is a different hire, not a competitor.

**Run all five discovery sweeps.** Each looks from an angle the others are blind to:

1. **The regulator's own directory** — licensing-board search for active, bonded, disciplinary-clean licensees serving the user's area. Catches the credentialed operator with no marketing budget.
2. **Map-pack and category listings** — Google Maps and Yelp category results filtered to the area, sorted by rating *and* by review count. Catches the incumbent that owns the market.
3. **Each major platform's top-rated** — one candidate per platform (Thumbtack, Angi, Nextdoor; G2, Capterra for software), because each platform's ranking has different blind spots and different pay-to-play exposure.
4. **Community word-of-mouth** — what neighbors and owner forums recommend when someone asks this exact question. Tier-4 evidence, discovery only, never load-bearing.
5. **The trade body's member list** — the industry association's directory for the region.

**Long list first, then the cut.** The sweeps should produce 10–20 names. Cut to 5–7 against these rules:

- Every survivor must plausibly pass the hard requirements — licensed, insured, serves the area, does this scope — and **be reachable today**.
- The field must **span the price band** — include the credible budget floor and one premium candidate.
- Include **at least one different-model challenger** — franchise vs owner-operator, big firm vs solo, agency vs freelancer — so the report has to argue for the model rather than assume it.
- **Any vendor the user named is always a candidate**, audited as honestly as its rivals.
- No more than **half the field from one platform**. A field one platform chose is that platform's ad inventory.

**Print the discovery.** The report states how many candidates were considered, where each finalist was found, and one line per long-list cut — "XYZ Roofing — cut: license expired March 2026."

## Source hierarchy

Work down this order and always prefer the higher tier. When a lower tier contradicts a higher one, the higher tier wins and the contradiction gets printed.

1. **Government and regulator records** — licensing boards, business registries, courts, permit history, tax and corporate filings. Use the equivalents for the reader's state and country.
2. **The vendor's own published material** — site, pricing page, written policies, warranty and guarantee text, terms, docs, changelog, status page. The written policy outranks what the salesperson says, every time.
3. **Established review platforms and accreditation bodies** — used through the trust tiers below, never flat.
4. **Recognised industry, trade and consumer bodies**, established cost guides (e.g. per-trade cost databases), reputable trade press. Directional; label as such.
5. **Forums, Reddit, social and blogs** — supporting colour only, never load-bearing, always labelled. Long-running local threads are often the only source of "they never came back" patterns — use for failure **modes**, never rates.

## Never cite

Content farms, SEO listicles, AI-generated roundups, affiliate "top 10" pages, lead-generation sites masquerading as reviews, anonymous claims, or anything you cannot open and read yourself.

Verify **every checkable claim** — license, bond, insurance, founding date, certifications, "as seen in" logos — and **every marketing claim**, one by one, against an independent record. **Surface every contradiction you find.**

## Vendor verification — the rules that decide this category

**The license number is the unit of analysis, not the business name.** Franchises, name-alike LLCs and re-registered businesses make names worthless. Verify the license in the board's own database, then check what other names it has traded under, whether past discipline attaches to it, and whether the "20 years in business" belongs to this entity or a conveniently dissolved predecessor. Say plainly when two candidates are actually the same operator.

**Insurance is a certificate, not a sentence on a website.** The report never treats "licensed and insured" as verified until the policy is confirmed — and the quote emails ask for the certificate **sent directly by the broker**, because a PDF from the vendor can be last year's.

**Availability is part of the answer.** A perfect vendor who cannot start for three months may be the wrong vendor. Check and print: taking new work, earliest start, and how it was confirmed — dated. An unreachable vendor is a finding.

**A quote is not a price.** Published ranges are directionally useful; only an itemized written quote against the exact scope is a price. Say which one every number is. This is why the identical-scope email section exists — quotes only compare when the scope is identical.

**Deposit and payment law.** Report the legal deposit cap and payment-schedule rules for the reader's state, with the statute named. What the vendor asks for up front versus what the law allows is one of the fastest red flags in the category.

**Complaint and discipline check on every candidate** — board discipline, court records, BBB complaint file — and print the result either way: "checked, nothing found" is information the reader cannot get from the vendor's website.

**Pooled reviews are a trap here too.** Multi-location companies and franchises pool reviews across branches. State whether the corpus belongs to **this location and this license**; if it can't be isolated, say so and downgrade.

**Re-open before you publish.** The day the report ships, re-verify the pick's and runner-up's license status and availability; chip anything that moved. If research spanned more than a day, re-check every finalist.

## Review platforms — rank them by how much they can be trusted

Decide the ranking during research, and print it as a matrix (template has the layout). Tiers:

| Tier | What earns it | Typical platforms |
|---|---|---|
| **T1 — Official record** | Government-run, legally accountable | Licensing board, court records, permit history |
| **T2 — Accountable** | Identity verified, vendor answers on the record | BBB (letter grade + complaint count + **response rate** — the response rate is the useful number), Google reviews with owner responses |
| **T3 — Open** | Real accounts, no verification, known incentive problems | Yelp — **including the hidden "not recommended" queue, where the pattern hides** — and Nextdoor |
| **T4 — Pay-to-play** | The platform sells placement to the vendors it ranks | Thumbtack, Angi, HomeAdvisor; sponsored slots on G2/Capterra grids | 
| **T5 — Never cite** | Affiliate lists, AI roundups, SEO farms, lead-gen sites | — |

Rules:

- **Volume never beats tier.** 400 Thumbtack reviews do not outrank one licensing-board disciplinary action. Say so whenever the raw numbers invite the opposite conclusion.
- **T4 is usable for discovery and volume, flagged, never for ranking.**
- **A rating with no readable review text is a number, not evidence.** Quote at least one verbatim review per platform you lean on, with date and attribution.
- **How the owner responds to bad reviews is a finding.** Quote one response verbatim — defensiveness under criticism predicts behaviour under a dispute.
- **Software hires**: T2 becomes verified-user G2/Capterra/TrustRadius reviews plus the vendor's status page and issue tracker; the sponsored placements on the same grids are T4.
- **When tiers disagree, the higher tier wins and the disagreement gets printed.**

## Citation format

**Cite inside each section, at the point of the claim.** Any figure, rating, legal rule, price range or company fact carries its source right there.

**Under every source, one italic line in parentheses** — what the source is, who runs it, why it's authoritative here, and the date read:

> Source: **CSLB — License Detail #1119278** · [cslb.ca.gov](https://www.cslb.ca.gov)
> *(California Contractors State License Board — the state agency that issues, renews and disciplines contractor licences; the authoritative record for licence status, bond and workers' comp. Read 27 Jul 2026.)*

**Every citation carries the date it was read.** The full **numbered source list still goes at the end**, grouped by type, including the sources that failed. **When a source is weak, say so in the italic line.**

## Rules — no exceptions

- **Label, don't assert.** Tag anything unproven `Verified` / `Unconfirmed` / `Not verified` / `Ask`.
- **A failed registry search is not proof something doesn't exist.** Say so in the report.
- **An empty platform is meaningless, not a red flag**, when that platform doesn't serve this category.
- **Date-stamp every fact.**
- **A filtered or anonymous review is never a finding.**
- **Volume never beats method or tier.**
- **Print your own dead ends** — with the phone number or URL for the reader to close them out themselves.
- **Be generous to the candidates you reject.** A vendor who conflicts with the reader's priority is wrong for them, not bad.
- **If the reader named a vendor they're leaning toward, audit it honestly** — including the case that they should not hire it.
